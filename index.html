Selecting a Topic

What do you wanna talk about?

A good research topic is a topic that actually interests you! Be honest with yourself and choose wisely!

If you don't care, why should anybody else?

If you aren't sure what matters to you, we can LIVE, LAUGH, LOVE our way to an answer!

Researchers are mindful about the nutritional value of ideas they're digesting. Select your favorite idea in each column. Then, before you chow down, consider the following criteria:

  • Expectations: What do you already know about this topic? How much do you still need to learn?
  • Appeal: Why does this topic interest you?
  • Timeline: How long are you willing to work on this topic?

At this point, you should have three worthwhile ideas for research topics. Good job! Unfortunately, a topic is only the starting point of an inquiry. Before we transform your topic into a research question, we'll need to acquire some basic information.

Choosing a Database

Think about your choice.

A subscription database is a pay-to-access, web-based collection of well-organized, critically reviewed scholarly information and materials.

A database will often focus on a particular discipline or subject area. They contain navigational aids that help researchers familiarize themselves with current scholarship and discover academic materials.

Why use a database?

Trustworthy

Peer-reviewed

Information from trusted sources and experts.

Private

No data-harvesting

No trackers, no algorithm, no nonsense. (though some offer personalized curation!)

Current

Regular updates

Databases keep up with rapidly changing fields.

Selective

Curated information

Databases contain smaller quantities of high-quality content than other web-based sources.

How to choose

Step One

Identify Your Subject

Review the available databases and decide which seem most relevant to your topic.

Step Two

Read and Rank

Read the descriptions for each relevant database. Rank the option(s) that best suit your topic.

Step Three

Sign In and Sign Up

Visit your top-ranked database, signing in if necessary. Be sure to create an account to ensure you stay organized and don't lose progress.

Preliminary Research

Just give it to 'em straight.

Establishing a Baseline

Before you begin researching in earnest, you need to establish a baseline understanding of your subject. Are you already familiar with the basic facts, fundamental tenets, and broad contours of the field?

  • Can you share a summary of a historical period or event?
  • Can you identify the major themes or preeminent literary devices of a fictional text?
  • Can you explain a scientific principle to a peer?
  • Can you break down the steps of a mathematical procedure?
A

Curated Lists and Pathfinders

Click your way through the curated resources on your database's home page. These tools can help you to get up to speed on an unfamiliar topic and inform future research. Look for phrases like:

  • Topic Lists
  • Subject Guides
  • Overviews
  • Timelines
  • Featured Resources
  • Popular Materials
B

Simple Searches

If you want to dive right in, perform a simple keyword search for your subject. Treat your database as if it were a search engine (like Google) and see what you turn up.

Focus on secondary sources like encyclopedia entries, introductory articles, and topic overviews.

Read widely, not deeply. (That comes later!)

Formulating a Question

This is the part you'll hate now, but adore down the line.

Research Problems

Once you have a firm grasp on your topic, it's time to identify a research problem for your project to address.

A research problem is a broad examination of a problematic or concerning area suitable for academic inquiry. It provides background and context for a pressing issue. These are descriptive—not prescriptive—statements. It's far easier to prescribe a course of action once you've described the state of a relevant field!

Research problems can be practical, addressing something people actually do or use, or theoretical, relating to the way people think about something. Which approach seems more relevant to your topic?

Try This

Build a Research Problem

In 150–250 words, work through these five moves. The counter below tracks your length.

  1. Identify your topic. One sentence.
  2. Add context. What do you already know? One or two sentences.
  3. Pick an issue. Describe a particular problem or debate. One sentence.
  4. Consider the unknown. What further knowledge seems necessary? One sentence.
  5. Prove relevance. Tell us why we should care. One sentence.

Problems vs. Questions

Research problems describe the state of a field to point out gaps in our knowledge; a research question fills an identified gap. Problems state what; questions speculate on how.

A research question identifies the specific information or understanding we need to resolve a key problem or debate. These inquiries place clear boundaries around a much wider subject to chart a course for investigation or analysis.

Your research problem likely prompts several questions. You must determine which of these inquiries you want to pursue. What question proves most appealing given your primary goal as a researcher?

Research Goals

As you craft a research question, consider your research goal. Research goals are not exclusive, but should be attainable! While your work can serve several purposes, a narrow scope is key. Are you looking to…

  • …explore? Will your work describe characteristics, chart changes, cite causes, survey a field? Is it examining or investigating? Then it's an exploration!
  • …explain? Will your work examine relationships, roles, influences, functions, and impacts? Is it expounding or interpreting? Then it's an explanation!
  • …evaluate? Will your work weigh pros and cons, gauge effectiveness, seek improvements? Is it assessing or appraising? Then it's an evaluation!

Research Questions

Now that you're prepared, it's time to put it all together! Look over your research problem, then consider your research goal. Your research question should fill the niche between them.

Remember: a research question revolves around an open-ended inquiry stemming from a Why or How. Who, What, When, and Where questions can offer support and provide context, but lack necessary depth.

As a researcher, it's your job to create and support a thesis, not a reference text. Your work must be focused, clear, complex, and arguable if you wish to produce a significant contribution.

Research Questions Are…

Scope

Focused

Good arguments are narrow and brief, addressing ONE IDEA OR ISSUE at a time.

Language

Clear

Don't obfuscate! Be specific and use direct language.

Depth

Complex

Your question should require a paper-length answer.

Stance

Arguable

An argument can be proven true or false. Its veracity can be disputed.

Try This

Draft Your Research Question

Each step offers an opportunity for strategic revision. Work your question over as you proceed. Incremental improvements are the most sustainable sort!

  1. Work from an initial question.
  2. Consider your goal and potential audience.
  3. Narrow your focus.
  4. Watch your language.
  5. Revise with an eye toward arguability and complexity.
